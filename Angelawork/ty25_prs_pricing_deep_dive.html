PRS Pricing Deep-Dive: Transparency vs. Value

TY25 PRS Survey · ~312K pricing friction points from ~290K unique respondents · Full Season (Nov 2025 – Jun 2026)

Companion analysis to TY25 VoC Cross-Source Analysis. Focused exclusively on PRS pricing friction, decomposed into "Too Expensive" (value perception) vs. "Surprise / Transparency" (hidden fees, misleading pricing, unexpected charges).

Headline Findings

1. "Too Expensive" dominates at 66% of all pricing friction

205K friction points from ~199K respondents — customers know the price and think it's too much. The signal is overwhelmingly a value perception problem, not an information problem. This is consistent with the cross-source finding (89% value in the original analysis) and persists across the full season.

2. Transparency friction is real, more severe, and more emotional

61K friction points (~59K respondents) cite hidden fees, misleading pricing, unexpected charges, or unclear disclosure. While smaller in volume, transparency friction runs hotter: 78% high severity vs 69% for value, 8.5% negative sentiment vs 4.5%, and emotions like "deceived" and "distrustful" appear almost exclusively here.

3. The emotional signatures are different — and actionable

"Too Expensive" customers are frustrated but often resigned — they know the price, they pay it, they resent it. Transparency customers feel deceived — they didn't know what they'd pay, and their trust is damaged. The former is a pricing strategy problem. The latter is a UX and communication problem with a clearer fix path.

Transparency friction is disproportionately severe

78% of transparency frictions are rated high severity vs 69% for value. And negative sentiment runs nearly 2x (8.5% vs 4.5%). The reason: "Too Expensive" customers are largely completers — they paid and filed. Their frustration is post-hoc. Transparency customers often feel tricked — the emotional sting is sharper even if they ultimately completed.

Three distinct transparency failure modes

Late disclosure: "pricing transparency issue" + "unclear pricing disclosure" + "pricing confusion" (~4.8K) — price shown too late in the flow. Unexpected charges: "unexpected pricing charge" + "unexpected billing charge" + "unexpected charges" (~5K) — charges customers didn't expect at all. Perceived deception: "misleading pricing" + "deceptive pricing model" + "pricing deception" (~2.1K) — customers who feel actively misled. The first two are UX-fixable. The third is a brand trust risk.

"Pricing concern" alone is 91% of the value category

The generic "pricing concern" label dominates (187K). This is the LLM's catch-all for "it costs too much." The more specific sub-types — "pricing too high", "pricing barrier", "excessive pricing" — represent the more pointed complaints where customers name the price or compare to competitors. Only 2.4K cite "pricing increase" or "pricing change," suggesting most frustration is about the absolute level, not a delta from last year.

Ambivalence and resignation are the value fingerprint

"Ambivalent" (3.2K), "satisfied but concerned" (1.3K), "conflicted" (524), and "resigned" (177) are largely absent from transparency friction. These customers like the product but feel the price is unfair. They're the most churn-vulnerable: one competitor offering equivalent quality at lower cost converts them.

So What: Implications

For "Too Expensive" (Value) — 205K friction points

This is a pricing strategy problem, not a UX problem. Customers know the price. They paid it. They think it's too much. The emotional signature (ambivalent, resigned, satisfied-but-concerned) suggests these are customers who like TurboTax but would leave for equivalent quality at a lower price. The $40 RT fee and state filing fees appear repeatedly as specific triggers.

For "Surprise / Transparency" — 61K friction points

This is a fixable UX and communication problem. Three concrete failure modes: (1) Late disclosure — pricing shown at end of flow, not beginning. (2) Unexpected add-on charges — state filing fees, RT fees, Expert Assist charges appearing without warning. (3) Perceived bait-and-switch — free-to-paid conversion feeling deceptive. Each has a clear design intervention. The emotional payoff (reducing "deceived" and "distrustful") is high relative to the effort.

Bottom line

Transparency improvements have reduced the surprise signal from prior years (19% of total pricing friction, down from higher rates in earlier analyses), but 61K friction points is still a large addressable pool. The emotional toxicity of transparency failures — trust damage, deception perception — makes them worth prioritizing even at lower volume. Value perception is the bigger number, but the levers are strategic (pricing, packaging, competitive positioning), not tactical.
